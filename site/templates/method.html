How people are counted

Git histories are read on GitHub's servers. Author emails and names are hashed in memory with a throwaway salt, merged when they obviously belong to one person, and replaced by a number that only means something inside one repo. Nothing identifying is stored or published.

Definitions
  • Active people: anyone with at least one human commit on the default branch in the past 365 days.
  • People making half the commits: the fewest people who together made at least half of those commits. This is the bus factor.
  • Few active contributors: an active project where that number is one, or where two or fewer people committed.
  • Looks quiet: no human commit in 365 days.
  • Slowing down: human commits at least halved from the year before, among projects with 10 or more commits that year.
  • Unknown: we couldn't find or read the repository. This is never treated as healthy.
Limits of counting people
  • One person using two names and emails can count twice. Two people can only merge if they share an exact email or full name, so that is rare.
  • Bots are found by name and email patterns, which were checked by hand on a sample. Automation with a human-looking name counts as a person.
  • Co-authors in commit messages aren't counted. Whoever squashes a pull request can take the credit for it.
  • Monorepos can't be split by package.

What the data can't show

  • Git can't tell finished software from software nobody is looking after. In our hand check of past cases, {{ ctx.audit_line }}
  • The 2022 cohort leans towards projects already popular then, and repos deleted before our read can't be scored, so results describe survivors to some degree. {{ b.coverage.repos_unreadable|comma }} of {{ b.coverage.cohort_repos|comma }} repos couldn't be read. If they had all gone quiet in every test year, the quiet rate would be {{ '%.0f'|format(100 * b.coverage.base_rate_if_all_unreadable_were_quiet) }}% rather than {{ '%.0f'|format(100 * b.coverage.test_base_rate) }}%.
  • Download counts include CI and mirrors. They measure machines, not people.
  • Requirements are read from each package's latest release and end at the top {{ n_packages|comma }}.
  • Dates come from git, which records no push date, so a few commits can appear earlier than they became public.

Safety

The xz attacker looked for lone, tired maintainers of important projects. This site tries not to make that easier. There is no list ranked by weakness, no sort by health or forecast, no way to combine "looks quiet" with "few active contributors", and no names, usernames or repository links. Working data is encrypted. The weekly frozen forecasts are public so the record can be checked, and anyone can sort them. Their hash order only slows that down. The aim is to show where help and funding should go.
